# Methods: Harmonization of Guideline Grading Systems

*Draft methods section (JAMA style). Decision identifiers (D1–D14) refer to `evident/grading_data/decisions.csv` (rendered in `docs/harmonization_tables.md`); they are for internal traceability and would be removed or moved to a supplement before submission.*

## Grading Systems Survey

We surveyed the grading systems used by 389 medical societies. For each society, one representative guideline was identified, and the scales used for recommendation strength and for quality of evidence were recorded verbatim, together with an example of the notation. The initial records were generated by large language model–assisted extraction and web search and then reviewed by an investigator (J.K.). Societies whose assignment was flagged as uncertain or internally inconsistent were checked against the full text of a primary guideline document. When the representative document was a joint guideline, it was coded with the system of the document rather than that of the society (D11). A society was excluded if it did not author clinical practice guidelines, or if it published only position statements, technical standards, or research or regulatory requirements (D12). After these exclusions, 352 of the 389 societies (90.5%) remained.

## Target Scale

All systems were mapped onto the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.¹,² GRADE was chosen because it was the most frequently used system: 156 of the 352 included societies (44.3%) used GRADE or a GRADE adaptation. The harmonized record has the following fields:
- recommendation strength (strong or conditional);
- direction (for or against);
- certainty of evidence (high, moderate, low, or very low).

Statements that the issuing body explicitly left ungraded, such as good practice statements and expert consensus, were classified as ungraded. Statements that explicitly declined to recommend (insufficient evidence) were classified as no recommendation. An axis that a system did not grade was recorded as not reported, and no value was imputed (D9). The original grade was always retained alongside the harmonized values.

## Crosswalk Construction

We identified 64 native scales: 32 for strength and 32 for evidence. Each native code of each scale was mapped to a harmonized value in a code-level crosswalk (314 native codes). Each entry also records the code's native definition and the mapping's confidence (high, moderate, or low). When a mapping depended on a methodological choice, the entry also records an alternative value for sensitivity analysis (72 codes; 22.9%). The scales assigned to 277 of the 284 graded societies (97.5%) are in the crosswalk, 251 (88.4%) on both axes and 26 on one axis only; the remaining 7 use systems that do not grade recommendations (see below). This is coverage of the scales, not of printed labels; label parsing was tested on each society's notation examples. Because systems change between versions, the scale is assigned to each guideline and version rather than to the society (D10). Examples are the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) levels of evidence before and after 2015, and the World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry (WFSBP) grading before and after its 2023 revision.

The following rules were applied.

**Strength.** Only the top tier of a native scale was mapped to strong. All lower positive tiers were mapped to conditional; for example, European Society of Cardiology (ESC) class IIa and IIb, ACC/AHA class 2a, and Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) grade B (D1). This is consistent with GRADE's requirement that a strong recommendation apply to almost all patients.² One exception was made: United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) grade B was mapped to strong, because the USPSTF recommends the service for both grade A and grade B (D5). Recommendations against an intervention without a separate harm tier, such as ESC class III, were mapped to strong against (D4). Explicitly "open" or "optional" recommendations were mapped to conditional, with direction taken from the wording (D3), because they can be phrased in either direction (eg, "can be combined" vs "cannot be recommended"). The same rule was applied to IDSA/European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) grade C, which is defined as "insufficient evidence … or benefit does not outweigh the risk … optional." Some systems derive a letter grade from the level of evidence alone. Examples are Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine letter grades, Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) 50, and the system of Shekelle et al.³ In these systems, grade D (expert opinion or inconsistent evidence) was mapped to conditional (D6).

**Certainty of evidence.** Certainty was assigned from study design, following the GRADE starting levels.² Randomized evidence was mapped to high or moderate, according to the native definition. Comparative observational studies, both cohort and case-control, were mapped to low (D2). Case series and descriptive studies were mapped to very low. Native levels that mix designs were mapped to the predominant design, with the other reading as the alternative; examples are Oxford 2011 level 4 and ESC level C. Expert opinion was not retained as a separate category, because GRADE does not consider it a category of evidence; it was mapped to very low (D13).² Some letter scales have no separate very-low level, such as the historical American College of Chest Physicians scheme, in which C denotes "low or very low."¹ For these scales, C was mapped to low, with very low as the alternative. Scales that do have a fourth level (D) map C to low only; which variant a guideline uses is determined by the presence of a D level (D14). In 1 system, levels I to IV rate individual studies and only the letter grade appears with each recommendation (European Psychiatric Association). Here, certainty was derived from the letter grade's definition. In other letter-grade systems (Oxford, SIGN), certainty was taken from the evidence level printed with the grade; when no level was printed, certainty was recorded as not reported, because one letter spans several evidence levels.

**Direction.** Some systems encode a negative finding in the grade itself. Examples are WFSBP's "A−/B−," IDSA/ESMO grades D and E, and the "against" levels of directional arrow scales. For these systems, direction was taken from the grade, and the evidence level kept its certainty. A consistent negative finding from randomized trials therefore remained high certainty. In all other systems, direction was taken from the recommendation text.

**Nongrading systems.** Some scales do not grade recommendations. These include appropriateness criteria, magnitude-of-benefit scores (ESMO-MCBS), molecular target actionability (ESCAT), resource tiers, efficacy conclusions (International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society), and pharmacogenetic utility categories. Their strength was recorded as not reported (D7). An exception was made for line-of-treatment categories (Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments; International Society for Bipolar Disorders). These categories are explicitly defined by evidence level and a benefit-harm judgment, so they were mapped to strength.

**Consensus.** Panel agreement, such as Delphi votes and percentage agreement, was recorded in a separate field and never converted into strength (D8). A consensus statement that also carried an evidence grade kept that grade, with strength not reported (D9); only statements without any grade were classified as ungraded.

## Statistical Analysis

The primary analyses used the primary mapping. In a prespecified sensitivity analysis, every entry with an alternative value was replaced by that value, and the main estimates were recomputed. The sensitivity analysis covers, for example:
- ACC/AHA level B-NR mapped to moderate instead of low;
- ESC level C mapped to low instead of very low;
- intermediate strength tiers mapped to strong instead of conditional.

## Limitations of the Approach

The crosswalk is a harmonization, not an official GRADE conversion; mappings involving intermediate tiers are judgments. Scale assignment at the society level relied on a single representative guideline per society. To mitigate this, each guideline is coded with its own scale and version at extraction.
